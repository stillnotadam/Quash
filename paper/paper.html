Proof-Carrying Triage: Refuting AI-Generated Vulnerability Reports with Certificates a Maintainer Can Check

Classifiers collapse on grounded slop. Instead we refute a report's claim against the program and return a proof the maintainer can re-check with any SMT solver, and we show sound triage requires the report to carry its claim, not state it in prose.

Working draft, 2026-10-27. All quantities are computed from the released artifact (code and the curl corpus); the build script regenerates every number in this document.

Abstract

Volunteer maintainers of critical open-source software are being buried under plausible but false vulnerability reports produced with the help of large language models. The reports that now cause the most harm are not lazy fabrications: they cite real functions, real files, and real lines, and they defeat both repository-grounding checks and text classifiers. We ask what signal still separates these grounded reports from genuine ones, and we show that the answer is refutation: instead of scoring how a report looks, we try to prove its central claim impossible against the program. We formalize an incoming report as a claim (a decision point, a branch outcome, and a predicate asserted to hold there), compute the path condition to that decision point from GCC GIMPLE in static single assignment form, and ask a solver whether the claimed predicate can still hold. When it cannot, we return the solver's unsatisfiable core: a minimal set of guards in the maintainer's own code that make the report impossible, a certificate a human can check in seconds. We never compute an input, so the pipeline produces no exploit. We validate the method with a differential oracle built from real fix commits: a genuine vulnerability's claim should be irrefutable on the vulnerable version and refutable once the fix lands. On a corpus of 684 disclosed curl HackerOne reports and 41 compiled releases, we report three findings. First, every method that scales by surface features collapses on grounded reports: repository grounding passes 53.1% of known slop and wrongly flags 33.8% of genuine reports, a text classifier that separates older slop at AUROC 0.985 falls to 0.608 on 2026 grounded reports, and a coarse semantic refuter is actively unsound, refuting 35.6% of genuine CVEs. Second, precision buys soundness: a field-precise refuter, validated against real fix commits, never refutes a genuine bug on its own vulnerable version, and its refutations flip exactly when the fix adds the missing check. Third, and this is the crux, that precision cannot be recovered soundly from a report's prose: a sound refuter driven by claims mined from report text still loses a genuine CVE (a conditional credential-check skip whose dimension is enforced on other paths), because the bug lives in a path corner that dimension-level reading cannot see, even though the report names the exact function and condition in English. The consequence is constructive, not defeatist: if the submitter carries the claim in a machine-readable form (the function, the decision point, the field-level predicate), the same checker becomes sound, returns a certificate when it refutes, and spends human time only on claims it cannot decide. We specify that claim-carrying report format, build the checker, and show head to head on the real corpus that carrying the claim removes the one genuine report that prose triage rejected (genuine reports lost falls from one to zero), while the checker refutes authored slop claims with certificates. We prove a soundness theorem (no genuine report is refuted, on a characterized decidable fragment) and make every refutation a proof-carrying SMT certificate that any solver re-checks, so a maintainer need not trust our tool. We argue the format removes the economic basis of grounded slop. We release the defense and the labeled benchmark, but not the means to generate grounded slop.

1. The flood, and the turn to grounded reports

In July 2025 the curl project, whose library ships in effectively every connected device, described receiving a rising tide of AI-generated vulnerability reports and estimated that a seven-person security team was spending hours per report while roughly one in twenty submissions was genuine (Stenberg, "Death by a thousand slops", 2025). On 31 January 2026 curl ended its HackerOne bug bounty. By late 2026 the project reported that the earliest, crudest slop had become rare, while nearly every incoming report now involves AI assistance and a growing fraction is grounded: it cites code that exists and argues in the register of a real disclosure.

This turn matters because the defenses the community reached for first assume the slop is lazy. A grounding check asks whether the cited code exists. A text classifier learns the surface of past slop. Both are defeated by a submitter, human or model, who reads the target before writing. The question this paper answers is narrow and practical: once a report is grounded, is there any signal left that separates it from a genuine report short of a maintainer reproducing it by hand?

2. Threat model: grounded slop

We separate three submitter strategies. Naive slop cites code that does not exist or misquotes it; a grounding check catches it. Grounded slop cites real functions, files, and lines, and offers plausible but incorrect reasoning about them; it is what remains after the naive wave. An adaptive submitter additionally tunes the report to pass whatever automated filter the project runs. Our threat model is the grounded, filter-aware submitter. We assume the target is open source, so the submitter and the triager see the same code. We assume the triager has bounded time and wants, for each report, either a cheap reason to reject it or a decision to spend human effort.

A defense is sound for our purposes if it never rejects a genuine report. Soundness is the property that matters to a maintainer: a false rejection is a missed vulnerability, which is worse than a slow triage. Everything below is evaluated first on soundness and only then on how much slop it removes.

3. The curl corpus

We collected every publicly disclosed curl report on HackerOne (684 reports) and labeled each from project data rather than opinion. A report is genuine if it is linked from a curl CVE advisory (148 reports), slop if it appears on the maintainer's public list of AI slop (49), resolved if curl fixed it without a CVE (15), and invalid otherwise (472: not-applicable, informative, duplicate, or spam). We compiled 41 curl releases from source so that each report can be analyzed against the version current when it was filed.

The gist of known slop is small (49 reports) because it is hand-curated; the much larger 2025 to 2026 invalid class is where grounded, AI-assisted reports accumulate. We therefore treat the gist as a clean slop sample and the recent invalid class as the grounded population that a triager actually faces.

4. Why every scalable defense collapses

4.1 Existence grounding (the MINT-style check)

The first-line defense asks whether a report's cited code exists. We implement a scoped grounding check: it extracts cited paths, functions, lines, and code snippets, drops the reporter's own proof-of-concept files and system headers, and verifies the rest against the exact revision the reporter targeted. The result is the trap in one number: 53.1% of known slop passes grounding fully (it cites real code), while the check wrongly flags 33.8% of genuine reports as ungrounded, because genuine reporters paste stack traces, library symbols, and their own harness code that does not resolve to the target tree. Grounding is neither sound nor effective against grounded slop.

4.2 Text classification

We train a balanced TF-IDF logistic classifier (a stand-in for the RoBERTa triagers reported in the literature) to separate genuine from invalid, and evaluate it three ways. It separates genuine reports from the curated slop gist almost perfectly (AUROC 0.985). Against the full invalid class it falls to 0.745. Against 2026 invalid reports, trained only on pre-July-2025 data (a realistic temporal split), it falls to 0.608, near chance. The most invalid-indicative tokens it learns are exactly the words of a security report ("overflow", "vulnerability", "buffer overflow", "proof"), which is why it cannot survive submitters who write like genuine reporters.

4.3 Reachability

A stronger, semantic filter asks whether the cited sink is reachable from a public entry point. We build a whole-program call graph from GCC call-graph dumps, resolve indirect calls by first-layer type matching, and check reachability from the library's public API and the command-line tool's entry. The call graph is sound against ground truth: of 2,355 functions the curl test suite actually executes, all but one are statically reachable. But reachability is a weak filter, because about 80% of curl's compiled functions are reachable from the public API. It catches only 12.2% of slop (reports that cite test servers, platform-gated backends, or dead code) while flagging just 1.4% of genuine reports. Reachability is nearly sound but removes little.

4.4 Coarse semantic refutation collapses into unsoundness

The natural next step is to refute a report semantically: match its claimed dimension (a host, a credential, a TLS setting) against a function that enforces equality in that dimension, and reject the report if the enforcement is present. This is where our own method, applied coarsely, falls into the grounded trap. A genuine report names a real dimension, and some real matcher in the code enforces some field in that dimension, so a keyword-level refuter rejects it. Measured over 364 in-scope reports, coarse refutation refutes 35.6% of genuine CVEs and 0.0% of slop: worse than useless, and unsound. The lesson sets up the rest of the paper: semantic refutation is only safe if it is bound to the exact claim, not a keyword.

5. CRDS: claim refutation via decision-point satisfiability

We formalize an incoming report as a claim, a triple (D, b, φ): a decision point D in a function (a branch, a match function's success return, or a sink call), the outcome b under which the bug manifests, and a predicate φ over program values that the report asserts holds when D takes outcome b. We compute the path condition PC(D,b) to that decision point from GIMPLE in SSA form, modeling the guards along the way. A claim is refuted when PC(D,b) ∧ φ is unsatisfiable on every path; the solver's unsatisfiable core is a certificate, naming the guards in the maintainer's own code that make the claim impossible. Otherwise the result is cannot refute, and the report is escalated to a human.

Soundness by construction. Every value we do not model (a load, a call result, a field) becomes a free symbolic variable. Free variables over-approximate reachable states, so a guard we fail to model can only make the query more satisfiable, that is, cause us to abstain. A missing guard never produces a false refutation. We also never ask the solver for a satisfying assignment, so no input, and no exploit, is ever produced.

5.1 A formal account

Let a decision point D be reached by a finite set of control paths Π(D). For a path π we build an over-approximate symbolic path condition PC#(π): a conjunction of the guards along π that we model, with every unmodeled subterm a fresh free variable. The claim predicate is φ.

Encoding lemma. For every concrete execution that reaches D along π in state σ, we have σ ⊨ PC#(π). (We assert only guards the code actually enforces; free variables admit every value, so no concrete reaching state is excluded.)

Refutation rule. The checker reports REFUTED iff for every π ∈ Π(D), PC#(π) ∧ φ is unsatisfiable.

Soundness theorem. If the checker reports REFUTED, no concrete execution reaches D in a state satisfying φ. Proof. Suppose one did, along some π. By the lemma its state satisfies PC#(π), and by assumption it satisfies φ, so PC#(π) ∧ φ is satisfiable, contradicting the rule. Corollary (no false rejection). A genuine report describes a concrete execution reaching D with φ; by the theorem it is never REFUTED. The cost of a false rejection, a missed vulnerability, is therefore never paid.

Relative completeness. On the fragment where Π(D) is finite (loop-free or bounded decision logic) and φ lies in linear integer arithmetic with equality, the checker refutes whenever the modeled guards entail ¬φ, because it enumerates Π(D) and the theory is decidable. The curl reuse matchers (straight-line gate chains) and the bounds sinks (directly guarded copies) lie in this fragment.

Two honest assumptions. Soundness rests on (i) faithful guard extraction and the comparator-equality axioms (a comparator we treat as forcing equality must actually do so; audited for the curl set), and (ii) finite Π(D): loops are handled by bounded unrolling, and claims whose decision point sits behind unbounded iteration fall outside the characterized fragment rather than being silently decided. Both are stated, not hidden; neither is a free variable's fault.

The same frame covers three claim families by varying φ:

  • Spatial bounds: φ is a size relation, for example a length can exceed a fixed buffer at a copy. A fix that adds a bounds check makes φ unsatisfiable on the guarded path.
  • Equality and reuse: φ asserts two records differ in a dimension at a point where a match function reports success. If the match forces equality in that dimension, reuse across it is refuted. This is the largest class of genuine curl vulnerabilities (connection-reuse and configuration-match bugs).
  • Reachability: φ is reachability of the claimed sink from an entry point, refuted by the absence of a call path.

We implement the path-condition encoder on GIMPLE directly and resolve match functions over two symbolic records, modeling curl's equality comparators (Curl_peer_same_destination, Curl_safecmp, blobcmp, curl_strequal and similar) as predicates whose truth on the success path forces equality of their arguments. The modeling assumption, that these comparators imply equality of the compared fields, holds for the curl comparators and is documented as a limitation, not hidden.

As a concrete, safe example, a slop report claimed a stack overflow of a fixed 2048-byte buffer through the telnet option handler. CRDS refutes the claim: on the path that reaches the copy, the maintainer's own length check forces the relevant length below 1000, so the claimed condition is unsatisfiable, and the unsatisfiable core is that check. A control claim that names a bound the code does not enforce correctly returns cannot refute, rather than bluffing. That asymmetry, refute only what the guards truly force, is the whole method.

6. The differential fix-commit oracle

How do we know a refuter is sound, not just confident? We use the project's own history. For a genuine vulnerability with a fix commit, the claim should be irrefutable on the vulnerable version (the reuse-key gap or missing bound is present) and refutable on the fixed version (the fix adds the check). Refuting a genuine claim on its own vulnerable version is, by definition, an unsound rejection of a real bug. We extract from each fix commit the exact record field or guard it adds, compile the changed matcher at both the fix commit and its parent (materializing the source tree at each commit and borrowing a generated configuration header from an era-matched build), and run the refuter for that precise field on both.

The result is the paper's central law. At keyword granularity, coarse refutation was 35.6% unsound. At field granularity, over 27 connection-reuse CVEs, the refuter is never unsound on the bug field: every vulnerable parent returns cannot refute. Precision is what buys soundness. The refutations that do fire, fire correctly: in 3 cases the claim flips from cannot refute on the parent to refuted on the fix, exactly when the fix adds the missing equality (for example an OAuth bearer token, an issuer certificate, or a client-certificate blob entering the reuse key).

The honest reading of Figure 4 is that sound refutation is narrow. It certifies the subset of claims that reduce to a field equality in a reuse key or a size relation at a sink. The remaining fixes change a boolean capability flag, restructure allocation math, or fix a use-after-free; these are real vulnerabilities whose claims our equality-and-bounds calculus cannot express, so the refuter correctly abstains rather than guessing.

7. The prose ceiling: why sound refutation cannot be mined from report text

The differential oracle validates the checker when it is handed the right claim. A triager, though, gets prose. So we tested the strongest sound prose-driven refuter we could build: compute, per release, the set of destination dimensions the connection-reuse key unconditionally enforces (composing a sound under-approximation over the mandatory gate chain of the top matcher), extract every dimension a report could be about with high recall, and refute only when every such dimension is already enforced, so that no unenforced dimension remains in which a real bug could live. Over 122 reuse-class reports this refutes 5 of 87 invalid reports and no slop, and it still loses one genuine CVE.

Worked example: CVE-2026-19931. The report claims that a connection authenticated with ambient Negotiate credentials is reused for a request with different explicit credentials. The reuse key does compare credentials and origin, so at the granularity a prose reader recovers ("credentials", "host"), both dimensions look enforced, and the sound dimension-level refuter rejects the report. But the real bug is a conditional: the credential comparison in url_match_auth_nego is skipped when conn->creds == NULL. The vulnerability lives in a path corner that dimension-level reading cannot see, while the dimension is enforced on other paths. Crucially, when we point the same engine at the function the report names in English, it gives the sound answer: forced_fields(url_match_auth) = ∅ on 8.21.0, no credential field is unconditionally forced, so the checker does not refute and the genuine report is preserved. The information needed for soundness is in the report; it is just in prose, where the machine cannot bind to it.

This is the ceiling. Sound refutation needs the field-and-path-precise claim; prose yields at best a dimension, and dimension-level refutation is unsound on exactly the bugs that hide in conditional corners of an otherwise-enforced dimension. No amount of better text mining fixes this, because the needed object (which decision point, which field, which path) is not reliably present as structure in free text.

8. Claim-carrying vulnerability reports

The fix is to move the claim out of prose and into the submission. A claim-carrying report attaches a small machine-readable claim: the target version, the function and decision point, and the field-level predicate asserted to hold there (optionally a witness the checker can run in a sandbox). The checker then returns one of three dispositions:

  • Refuted, with the unsatisfiable core as a certificate: a precise but false claim (grounded slop) lands here, with a proof in the maintainer's own code.
  • Confirmed, if a carried witness reproduces on the target and not on the fixed build.
  • Escalate, when the claim cannot be refuted and no witness reproduces: the claim names an unenforced path, as a genuine bug does, or lies outside the decidable fragment. Human time is spent only here.

Pointed at a carried claim rather than at prose, the checker is sound by the construction of Section 5, and the worked CVE above is preserved rather than lost. The format (a JSON schema, released with the artifact) is deliberately small; it asks the submitter for exactly the object the checker needs and nothing more.

The reference checker. We implement the checker (ccvr_check.py, released). It takes a claim-carrying report, locates the named function at the named version, and returns a disposition with a certificate. Run on three claims it gives exactly the intended behavior:

The two slop reports are real entries from the maintainer's AI-slop list, authored into the claim format from the function and buffer they themselves name; the checker refutes them with certificates. The two genuine CVEs, carried the same way, are escalated rather than refuted, including CVE-2026-19931, the report prose triage lost in Section 7, and a famous overflow on its own vulnerable version. The pattern is the whole mechanism: a precise but false claim earns a proof of its falsity, a precise true claim earns a human. We do not synthesize any input in either case; the overflow rows are decided from guards alone.

Corpus-scale soundness. We ran the two binding strategies head to head over all 121 reuse-class reports: prose binding (refute when every mentioned dimension is enforced somewhere by the reuse-key chain, the best sound prose-only refuter) versus claim-carrying binding (bind to the decision-point function the report names). Prose binding refutes 5 of 86 invalid reports but loses one genuine CVE (CVE-2026-19931). Claim-carrying binding loses zero genuine reports. That is the soundness win, measured on the real corpus: moving the binding from mentioned-dimension to carried-decision-point removes the false rejection.

An honest caveat in the same breath: when claims are mined from existing prose this way, the refutation yield is near zero, because the reuse reports in the corpus were never written to carry a precise decision point, so the checker has nothing precise to bind to and escalates. This is not a weakness of the checker; it is the reason the format must change. Precision has to be authored by the submitter, as in the worked claims above and the fix-commit-derived claims the differential oracle validates, not retrofitted onto prose. The corpus-scale result is therefore a soundness result (zero genuine lost under carried binding), and the refutation power is demonstrated on authored claims (the three dispositions above, and the three CVEs that flip in the oracle).

8.1 Proof-carrying verdicts

A REFUTED verdict should not ask the maintainer to trust our tool. So the checker emits each refutation as a standalone SMT-LIB proof obligation plus the source guards (file and line) it was built from. Anyone re-runs the obligation with any SMT solver and confirms it is unsatisfiable; our checker re-verifies every proof it emits in a fresh solver context (reported as verified-unsat), and the named guards let a maintainer check that the obligation faithfully reflects their code. The trusted base shrinks from "our analysis pipeline" to "an off-the-shelf solver plus a handful of named guards." For the telnet overflow the obligation is, in essence:

; the path guards and the report's claim, together, are unsatisfiable
(declare-fun strlen_subopt_ttype () Int)
(assert (not (< 1000 strlen_subopt_ttype)))   ; guard at telnet.c: reject length > 1000
(assert (< 1000 strlen_subopt_ttype))          ; the report's claimed overflow condition
(check-sat)                                    ; => unsat

Any solver returns unsat; the certificate stands on its own. This is proof-carrying code's idea applied to vulnerability triage: the verdict travels with a proof the recipient can re-check without trusting the producer. We are not aware of prior triage work whose rejections are independently verifiable artifacts.

The economic argument. Grounded slop is cheap today because writing a convincing paragraph about real code is cheap. Under claim-carrying triage the submitter must instead supply a precise, checkable claim. A false precise claim is refuted automatically, with a certificate, at no maintainer cost, so it earns nothing. An unfalsifiable or vague claim fails the format and is quarantined, again at no cost. The only claim that reaches a human is one the checker can neither refute nor confirm, which is exactly a claim about an unenforced path: either a genuine bug, or a slop report that has done the work of pointing at a genuine gap, which is the work of finding a bug. Claim-carrying does not decide truth; it makes slop as expensive to produce as the real thing, which is what protects maintainer time.

9. The residual

Even with carried claims, the reports the checker can neither refute nor confirm form a residual: logic errors outside the bounds and reuse-key fragments, capability-flag regressions, use-after-free and lifetime bugs, protocol confusions. For these there is no cheap certificate and a maintainer must reproduce. CRDS carves off, soundly and with a proof, the slice where human time is not required, routes reproducible claims to a witness check, and names the rest precisely. That triage partition, every report receiving a justified disposition, is the deployable result.

10. Related work and the obvious question

Validating tool alerts. VulnSAGE (2026) adjudicates static-analysis alerts by writing an LLM's reading into a claim structure and checking its premises on a code-property graph, then reproducing. LLM4PFA uses an LLM to write path constraints and a solver to judge feasibility. EVIDENT discharges analyzer warnings with a verified harness. All take tool alerts as input and most judge with a graph query or LLM-authored constraints rather than a checkable unsatisfiable core from compiler IR.

Reproducing reports. Industrial systems (Elastic's 2026 triage pipeline among them) reproduce incoming bug-bounty reports in sandboxes and agree with human triage around 85% of the time. Reproduction is complementary to refutation: reproduction confirms the true positives, refutation discharges a class of false positives with a proof and no execution.

Triaging reports by surface. Text-only triagers over HackerOne reports reach high accuracy on historical labels but, as their authors note, provide no way to separate machine-written false claims from genuine ones. Our Section 4 quantifies why on grounded data.

Why not gcc -fanalyzer? GCC's analyzer is symbolic execution over GIMPLE and finds bugs. It does not take an external claim and return a minimal set of the program's own guards that refute it. CRDS is not a bug finder; it is a claim refuter whose output is a certificate addressed to a specific report. The novelty we claim is the combination: incoming reports as input, a GIMPLE-derived certificate as output, and fix commits as the validation oracle. We explicitly do not claim to be first to validate claims with program analysis, first to filter false positives with a solver, or to produce a general proof of non-exploitability; a CRDS certificate holds for the chosen entry point, loop bound, and memory model, and we say so.

11. Ethics and responsible release

This work studies a flood that a careless release could worsen. We therefore release the defense (the refuter, the call-graph and reachability tools, the corpus, and the differential oracle) and the labeled benchmark, and we do not release any means of generating grounded slop. Every report we study is already public and already disclosed; we submitted nothing to any live program. Our analysis produces verdicts and certificates only: at no point does the pipeline synthesize an input, so it cannot be turned into an exploit generator. We frame the tool as defense-first and recommend coordinating with affected maintainers before any operational deployment against a live queue.

12. Limitations and threats to validity

  • Single project. All results are on curl. curl is an unusually good subject (public reports, a public slop list, fix commits, a strong test suite), but generality is unproven. The method is not curl-specific, but the comparator library and the dimension vocabulary would need porting.
  • Modeling assumptions. We treat named comparators as implying field equality and treat unmodeled values as free. The first could in principle be wrong for a comparator we misclassify (we audited the curl set); the second only ever causes abstention.
  • Claim extraction. Turning prose into a formal claim is itself hard. Our automatic extraction is heuristic; the differential oracle sidesteps extraction by reading the fixed field from the commit, which is why it is our soundness instrument rather than a deployed triager.
  • Reach. Sound refutation covers a minority of grounded reports. We present this as a measured boundary, not a solved problem.
  • Diversity of fix shapes. The equality and bounds fragments cover reuse-key and spatial claims; the differential oracle flips cleanly on the reuse CVEs whose fix adds a field (or packed bitfield group, which we now resolve via the XOR-mask idiom) to a match function, but most reuse fixes live in clone, setup, or follow logic rather than in the matcher, and fall outside the fragment. This is a boundary of the fragment, not of the checker's soundness.
  • The claim-carrying format is a proposal, not a deployment. We specify it, release the schema, and show the checker gives the sound verdict on a carried claim, but we have not run a live bug-bounty program on it, so adoption behavior and the submitter experience are untested. The economic argument is a mechanism-design argument, not a measured outcome.

13. Conclusion

The grounded-slop flood defeats every triage signal that reads a report's surface, and it defeats semantic refutation too unless that refutation is bound to the exact claim and proven against the program. The decisive obstacle is that the exact claim cannot be recovered soundly from prose: the bugs that matter hide in conditional corners of dimensions that look enforced, and a genuine report describes them in English that a machine cannot bind to. The way through is not a cleverer classifier, which cannot exist, but a change of format: let the report carry its claim, and refutation stops being a guess and becomes a proof, a short checkable reason in the maintainer's own code that a report cannot be what it says. That proof is cheap to verify and impossible to fake, and it makes slop as expensive to produce as a real bug. The honest result is not a machine that knows which reports are true; it is a protocol under which the only reports that reach a human are the ones that have earned the attention, which is what protects the scarce time of the people who keep the internet's plumbing alive.
